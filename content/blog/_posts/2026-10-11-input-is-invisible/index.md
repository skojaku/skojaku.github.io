During the YRCCS2026 warm-up session, Laurent Hébert-Dufresne gave a presentation on “How to Read a Research Paper.” Inspired by that, I’d like to take this opportunity to summarize some thoughts I’ve been having.

## Society Evaluates Output, Not Input

Society evaluates output. If you create something, you receive compensation in return. That is how society works. That is why many researchers, myself included, have spent so much time on output.

If you write a paper, it counts as an achievement. Papers are sent for peer review, and the very act of writing one is a plus for your career.

On the other hand, there is no reward for reading. Nowhere is it recorded how many papers you’ve read. As a result, reading can feel like a waste of time.

To be honest, I don’t read many papers. I read papers I’ve been asked to review, but I don’t have the habit of actively seeking out and reading papers on my own. There’s no doubt that the amount of input I receive falls far short of that of researchers active on the front lines, and I think I’ve read fewer papers thoroughly than even my students have lately.

## There Is a Delay in the Effects of Input

The relationship between the input of ideas and their output can be likened to a steam engine.

In a steam engine, firewood is burned to heat water. The water turns into steam and is trapped inside the boiler. The trapped steam gradually builds up pressure as it pushes against one another. Only when the pressure exceeds a certain threshold does the piston begin to move. Even if you feed more firewood into the boiler, the piston doesn’t move immediately.

We, too, are thinking engines that work in the same way. We acquire materials (input) such as books, research papers, and conversations with others. These accumulate little by little in our minds, building up like pressure. When that pressure exceeds a certain threshold, it emerges as writing or ideas. Simply reading does not generate output; it is only through the process of thinking, writing, and speaking that what has accumulated is released.

The difference from a steam engine is that if you allocate time toward output, the volume of output (such as the number of papers) increases. This is problematic. Why? Because while the person producing the output receives short-term rewards for their work, if they continue to produce similar ideas, those rewards will dwindle over the long term. If the substance of your ideas is becoming repetitive, that is a warning sign.

## Making It a Habit

While reading papers and listening with interest to others’ ideas and stories is important, the asymmetry in rewards between input and output (as mentioned earlier) and the delay in response make it difficult to maintain a steady flow of input.

One researcher is tackling this problem with a personal approach. Molecular biologist Olivia Rissland began reading one paper every day starting January 1, 2018. She says it started as “a bit of a lark,” but within a month, it had become a habit. By June 2020, she had read a paper every day for 899 consecutive days. She shares the papers she reads in a dedicated Slack channel for her lab and records each one, line by line, in a Google Sheet. She believes that setting aside time to read papers is more important to the lab’s success than answering emails. (Source: [Nature Index, September 8, 2020](https://www.nature.com/nature-index/news/this-scientist-read-research-academic-paper-every-day-what-she-learned))

Based on this article and Laurent’s comments, I’ll outline below what I can do, taking my own tendencies into account.

## How to Reduce the Cost of Input

Input takes time. Therefore, we need to find ways to reduce the cost of input. Recently, large-scale language models (AI like ChatGPT, trained on vast amounts of text) and agent-based AI (AI that independently constructs and executes work procedures) have become available. There are also an increasing number of systems designed to quickly understand text. By using these tools, we should be able to reduce the cost of input.

Therefore, I’ve decided to divide my approach to reading papers into two modes.

### Broad Reading Mode

This is a mode for gaining a broad, superficial understanding of the papers that come my way every day. I read the titles and abstracts and select the ones that interest me.

What I’m currently trying is turning papers into podcasts. Since I enjoy podcasts, I think this might allow me to hear slightly more detailed content, beyond what’s in the abstract, in audio form.

There are also several tools for selecting papers, such as Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, and arXiv RSS feeds. However, no matter which tool I use, narrowing down the selection remains a challenge. Right now, I’m building my own recommendation system by combining large language models with keywords. It is still in the testing phase.

### In-Depth Reading Mode

In in-depth reading mode, I actually read the paper from start to finish. However, it is hard to find time to read even when I try to set time aside. That’s why I’ve decided to set aside specific reading times in advance each week and make sure to stick to them regularly.

This is where the concept of “accountability,” as mentioned by Laurent Hébert-Dufresne, comes in handy. Reading papers at a set time with a group makes it easier to stick with something that might be hard to maintain on my own.

## Listening to Others

Papers aren’t my only source of input. New ideas also emerge from talking with people and listening to presentations.

Recently, there’s been an increase in online presentations and conversations. In terms of sheer volume of information, there may not be a significant difference between in-person and online interactions. Still, I feel that when I listen to a presentation in person, the content sinks in more deeply. It sticks in my memory better, and various ideas pop into my head while I’m listening.

I used to check my email on my computer while listening to presentations. That’s fine if the topic doesn’t interest me. However, I’ve come to feel that I should loosen my filter a bit and really listen to what people have to say. While some of it might be a waste of time, it can also plant the seeds for new ideas.

However, simply listening doesn’t make the information stick, so I take notes. Jotting down points that catch my attention helps me stay focused, and the act of writing itself expands my thinking further. Listening in person and taking notes is something I plan to make a habit going forward.

## Summary

Output and input are asymmetrical in terms of evaluation. Output yields rewards, while input does not. Furthermore, because there is a delay before input is converted into output, it’s hard to feel the effects of input.

Moreover, if you devote time to output, you’ll produce some kind of result and gain short-term rewards.

However, output is supported by input. If input decreases, output will eventually dwindle as well.

Of course, this doesn’t mean you should spend all your time on input. You need to continue producing output to some extent. Therefore, the key is figuring out how to lower the cost of input and make it a habit within those constraints.

Just as a steam train takes in firewood and water as inputs, converts them into power using various mechanisms, and turns its wheels, we are thinking engines that take in inputs (the work of others), transform them, and create new outputs. With that image in mind, I want to invest in inputs with a long-term perspective.
