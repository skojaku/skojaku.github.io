At the YRCCS2026 warm-up, Laurent Hébert-Dufresne gave a presentation on how to read papers. It got me thinking, and I want to write down what I have been thinking about.

## Society rewards output, not input

Society rewards output. If you make something, you receive a reward for it. This is how society works. So, like many researchers, I have spent a lot of time on output.

If you write a paper, it counts as part of your record. The paper goes to peer review, and simply having written it helps your career.

Reading, on the other hand, earns no reward. Nobody records how many papers you have read. As a result, reading can feel like a waste of time.

To be honest, I do not read many papers. I read the papers I am asked to review. For papers I find myself, I do not have a habit of actively searching and reading. My intake is certainly far below that of researchers who are active at the front line. I also think I have read fewer papers carefully than my students have lately.

## Input has a delay before it shows up as output

The relationship between input and output of ideas can be compared to a steam engine.

In a steam engine, we burn firewood to heat water. The water turns into steam, which is trapped inside the boiler. The trapped steam pushes against itself little by little and builds up pressure. Only when the pressure passes a certain level does the piston move. Adding firewood does not make the piston move right away.

We think in the same way. We take in materials (input) such as books, papers, and what other people say. In our heads, these build up little by little, like pressure. When the pressure passes a certain level, it comes out as writing or new ideas. Reading alone does not produce output. Only when we go through the process of thinking and then writing or speaking does what has built up come out.

The steam engine differs from us in one way. If you put more time into output, the amount of output (such as the number of papers) goes up. This is a bad trap. The person producing the output gets a short-term reward for each product. But if they keep producing similar ideas, the long-term reward shrinks. If the content of your ideas is becoming similar, that is a warning sign.

## Building the habit

Reading papers and listening carefully to other people's ideas matter. Still, because of the asymmetry in rewards and the delay between input and output, keeping up with input is not easy.

One researcher is dealing with this problem through her own practical methods. Olivia Rissland, a molecular biologist, began reading one paper every day on January 1, 2018. At first it was "a bit of a lark," but within a month it had become a habit. By June 2020, she had read papers for 899 days in a row. She shares the papers she reads in a dedicated Slack channel for her lab, and she records each one on a line in a Google Sheet. She considers that setting aside time to read papers matters more to her lab's success than replying to email. (Source: [Nature Index, September 8, 2020](https://www.nature.com/nature-index/news/this-scientist-read-research-academic-paper-every-day-what-she-learned))

Based on this article and Laurent's talk, I will describe below what I can do, given my own tendencies.

## Lowering the cost of input

Input takes time. So we need ways to lower its cost. Recently, large language models (AI trained on large amounts of text, such as ChatGPT) and agentic AI (AI that plans and carries out tasks step by step on its own) have become available. Tools that help us understand text quickly are also increasing. Using them should lower the cost of input.

That is why I decided to split my reading of papers into two modes.

### Reading broadly

This mode is for the papers that arrive every day. I read them broadly and at a shallow level. I read the titles and abstracts, and I pick the ones that interest me.

What I am testing now is turning papers into podcasts. I like podcasts, so I think I could hear more detailed content, which is not in the abstract, as audio.

There are several tools for finding papers, such as Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, and arXiv RSS feeds. But no matter which one I use, choosing from the results is hard. I am now building my own recommendation system that combines large language models with keywords. It is still in testing.

### Reading deeply

In deep reading mode, I read papers all the way through. Even so, it is hard to find time for it. So I set a reading time within each week in advance and keep it on a regular schedule.

This is where the idea of accountability that Laurent Hébert-Dufresne mentioned helps. When a group reads at a fixed time, it is easier to keep going than when I read alone.

## Listening to people

Input is not only papers. Talking with people and listening to their talks can also spark new ideas.

In-person talks have been replaced by online ones more and more. If you look only at the amount of information, there may not be much difference between in-person and online. However, I feel that listening to talks in person lets the content sink in more. It stays in my memory longer, and many ideas come to me while I listen.

I once checked email on my laptop while listening to a talk. If a talk does not interest me, that is fine. But I feel I should loosen the filter a little and listen to people more. It may sometimes be wasted, but it can also become the seed of a new idea.

Just listening makes things hard to remember, so I take notes. Writing down what catches my attention keeps me focused, and as I write, my thoughts spread further. I plan to make it a habit to listen in person and take notes.

## Summary

Input and output are asymmetric in terms of evaluation. Output earns rewards, and input does not. Also, because input takes time to turn into output, its effect is hard to feel. If I put more time into output, I can produce some work and earn a short-term reward. But output rests on input. If input shrinks, output will eventually thin out too.

Of course, it is not a good idea to spend all of my time on input. I still need to keep up output to some extent. So what matters is how to lower the cost of input and make it a habit, within those limits.

Just as a steam train takes in firewood and water, turns them into power through various mechanisms, and turns its wheels, we are thinking engines. We take in input (other people's work), transform it, and produce new work. With this picture in mind, I want to invest in input with a long-term view.
